Chapter 2
CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK
This chapter presents a collection of relevant local and foreign literature and studies that support the development of Facial Recognition and Attendance Monitoring with Embedded System (FRAMES): A Web-Based System with Hand Gesture Control using Raspberry Pi, along with digital room visualization for real-time classroom occupancy at the Technological University of the Philippines.

Review of Related Literature and Studies
The increasing integration of digital technologies in educational institutions has led to the development of systems that enhance monitoring and management of classroom activities. In response to the need for accurate, real-time attendance tracking and efficient room occupancy monitoring, researchers have explored technologies such as facial recognition, hand gesture authentication, and digital room visualization. These innovations are being explored to create a smarter, more efficient, and responsive learning environment.

Smart Monitoring in Educational Systems
	Smart monitoring in education emphasizes automation of attendance and classroom tracking by leveraging technologies such as IoT, embedded systems, and real-time analytics. A review by Rama Krishna et al. (2023) synthesized existing smart attendance systems using Raspberry Pi and highlighted common design strategies including camera-based recognition, web dashboards, and IoT integration. The review also identified key gaps such as the lack of multimodal authentication and limited evaluation of real-time performance. Similarly, Zhao, Zhao, and Qu (2022) introduced an IoT-based classroom attendance management framework that enabled centralized monitoring of student presence, integration of faculty schedules, and analysis of subject-based usage trends. Their study emphasized that smart monitoring extends beyond raw attendance logging, instead generating higher-level insights for resource allocation and administrative decision-making.
Swathi and RathnaChary (2023) advanced this concept by demonstrating a Raspberry Pi–driven attendance system that employed video-based face detection and recognition in classroom settings. The authors emphasized the efficiency of live monitoring pipelines and the ability of Raspberry Pi to support educational deployment at relatively low cost. In parallel, Ashok Kumar et al. (2021) explored deep learning–enabled attendance monitoring, combining Raspberry Pi with recognition models to achieve improved accuracy while maintaining feasibility for smart classroom use cases. These studies collectively show that “smart” monitoring is operationalized through features such as real-time dashboards, analytics, and automated logging, rather than behavioral surveillance.
From these insights, the present study defines smart monitoring in a scoped and practical sense: enabling entry, exit, and break tracking through facial recognition and gesture control, and producing subject-specific attendance reports, visual dashboards, and alerts for unknown individuals. Unlike prior work that focused solely on facial recognition or IoT integration, this capstone integrates multimodal fusion (face + gesture) and real-time visualization, ensuring the monitoring process is both accurate and actionable for students, faculty, and administrators. Moreover, effective smart monitoring requires not only reliable recognition but also secure and interpretable visualization. This involves the coordination of embedded hardware, computer vision models, and web-based interfaces that work together to collect, process, and present attendance data in real time.

Embedded Systems for Attendance Monitoring
Embedded systems serve as the backbone for low-cost, portable, and real-time attendance monitoring applications. By integrating hardware like Raspberry Pi with software pipelines for detection and recognition, they enable on-device processing and reduce dependency on external servers. Swathi and RathnaChary (2023) demonstrated such integration by using Raspberry Pi as the embedded platform for face detection and attendance management, highlighting its efficiency in managing live classroom data with minimal external infrastructure. Their findings underscore the Pi’s suitability for educational contexts where affordability and portability are key constraints.
Ashok Kumar et al. (2021) extended this perspective by employing deep learning models on Raspberry Pi to enhance recognition accuracy. The study balanced computational feasibility with accuracy by optimizing lightweight neural models for embedded deployment. This shows that embedded devices are not limited to simple feature-based recognition but can also handle optimized deep learning pipelines. More recently, Touzene, Abed, and Larabi (2024) introduced an Embedded Intelligent System for Attendance Monitoring, which coupled Raspberry Pi hardware with web-based dashboards. Their system emphasized modular design, capturing and processing attendance data directly on the Pi while synchronizing outputs to a central interface for administrators.
Collectively, these studies show that embedded systems can sustain attendance monitoring by combining low-power hardware, optimized recognition pipelines, and integrated web dashboards. For this capstone, the Raspberry Pi 4 is selected as the embedded device to balance cost and performance, enabling multimodal recognition (facial recognition + hand gesture control) and supporting web-based monitoring in real time. Unlike previous works, this study adds gesture-gated logging to reduce false positives and integrates administrative features such as report generation and unknown-user alerts, extending the typical role of embedded attendance systems toward more intelligent monitoring. While embedded systems ensure portability and on-site processing, their full effectiveness depends on the quality of the recognition pipeline. Thus, the following sections discuss the underlying technologies that enable the FRAMES prototype — facial recognition, gesture recognition, and multimodal fusion — all optimized for Raspberry Pi–based deployment.

Facial Recognition Techniques & Tools (Raspberry Pi–Friendly)
	Facial recognition on edge devices generally falls into two major categories: traditional feature-based methods (e.g., HOG + SVM or Haar cascades) and modern deep learning embedding pipelines (e.g., MobileNetV2 + FaceNet). The embedding-based approach offers improved accuracy and resilience, though it often demands greater computational power—something that needs careful design on Raspberry Pi platforms. AIP Conference research showed that classic detection methods using Haar or HOG can achieve real-time performance on Raspberry Pi 2, 3, and especially Pi 4, when input image widths are kept at or below 300 pixels. However, deeper detectors like SSD or MMOD exhibit much slower speeds and are unsuitable for real-time tasks without additional hardware acceleration (Nguyen et al., 2021).
The IoT-MFaceNet model, which combines MobileNetV2 with FaceNet embeddings and utilizes TensorFlow Lite quantization, was successfully deployed on a Raspberry Pi 400. The optimized pipeline delivered strong recognition performance, running at approximately 6 FPS in a multithreaded setup (Mohammad et al., 2024). The reported F1-scores approached 0.998–0.999, indicating near-perfect classification under controlled conditions. Another study applied a multitask learning (MTL) framework on Raspberry Pi, leveraging a MobileNet backbone to perform recognition alongside auxiliary tasks. The system demonstrated over 99% accuracy, proving that Pi 4 systems can handle multiple processing tasks efficiently when properly optimized (Aboluhom & Kandilli, 2023). A public implementation on Git Hub integrated ArcFace recognition with anti-spoofing and facial recording on Raspberry Pi 4, showing that spoof protection can be added with only around 40 milliseconds of overhead—making it feasible for real-time operation (Qengineering GitHub repository, 2023).
While accuracy is well-documented, many studies—especially IoT-MFaceNet and the MTL framework—omit detailed latency/FPS numbers unless optimized scenarios are specified. Anti-spoofing features are often demonstrated only in code repositories without full evaluation. Additionally, very few end-to-end embedding-based implementations have been rigorously evaluated on Raspberry Pi hardware. Given these insights, this study will deploy a quantized MobileNetV2 + FaceNet embedding model on Raspberry Pi 4, achieving an effective balance of accuracy and real-time performance. Each enrolled user will be represented by a 128-dimensional embedding vector to reduce storage burden while preserving recognition capability. The researchers may intend to integrate software-based anti-spoofing (e.g., blink or texture-based checks) coupled with gesture gating, drawing on the performance feasibility indicated by the ArcFace GitHub example.
Face detection refers to the process of identifying and localizing faces within an image frame—essentially answering “where is the face?” In contrast, face recognition goes a step further by answering “whose face is it?” (Liu et al., 2023). Detection only isolates the region of interest, while recognition extracts unique numerical representations (embeddings) from that region to compare against stored profiles. In FRAMES, detection is handled by a lightweight model to maintain real-time performance, whereas recognition uses embeddings for accurate identity validation.
Face embeddings are high-dimensional numerical vectors (typically 128-dimensional) generated by deep neural networks such as FaceNet or dlib’s ResNet-based model. Each embedding represents abstract facial features that remain consistent across lighting, angle, or expression changes (Schroff et al., 2015). These embeddings enable fast similarity comparison using Euclidean distance — smaller distances indicate higher likelihood of identity match. The FRAMES system uses dlib’s embedding generator to store and compare user embeddings in MySQL, ensuring minimal storage footprint while maintaining recognition reliability. A persistent challenge in facial recognition is spoofing — where printed photos or video replays are used to impersonate legitimate users. To mitigate this, FRAMES employs gesture gating as a behavioral liveness layer: even if a face image is presented, the system requires a corresponding gesture within a short time window. This ensures both liveness and intent, addressing common presentation-attack vectors without relying on complex 3D or infrared sensors (Mukthineni et al., 2020).
Although facial embeddings demonstrate strong resilience to variations in lighting and appearance, several environmental and physiological factors can influence recognition accuracy. The embedding process primarily encodes geometric relationships among facial landmarks—such as the spacing of the eyes, nose, and jawline—rather than raw color information, making it relatively robust across diverse skin tones. However, bias may still arise if the pre-trained models were developed using imbalanced datasets, underscoring the importance of uniform lighting and camera calibration in ensuring fair recognition (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018; “Facial Recognition Software Struggles to Detect Dark Skin,” 2023).
Facial occlusions such as masks present a more significant challenge, as they obscure key regions used for feature extraction, often leading to reduced confidence scores or partial mismatches (Boutros et al., 2021; Saoud et al., 2025). Recent studies on masked-face recognition propose the use of partial embeddings or retraining with masked datasets to address this limitation (Alzubi et al., 2025). In FRAMES, the issue is mitigated through multimodal fusion—combining face recognition with gesture confirmation—ensuring that even when facial visibility is limited, authentication remains secure and intentional.
Cosmetic changes, including makeup, generally affect surface-level textures but not the underlying landmark geometry, resulting in minimal impact on recognition accuracy. Nonetheless, consistent camera exposure and optional re-enrollment help maintain precision for users with frequent appearance changes. Overall, these design considerations affirm the robustness of embedding-based recognition while highlighting FRAMES’s adaptive verification strategy under real-world classroom conditions.

Hand Gesture Recognition for Control
Hand gesture recognition can generally be categorized into static gestures, which are fixed hand poses, and dynamic gestures, which involve motion over time. For low-power embedded devices such as the Raspberry Pi, static gestures tend to be more reliable and less computationally demanding. This makes them particularly suitable for simple control actions, such as logging in, logging out, or placing the system on break. As highlighted by Mohamed, Hassan, and Jamil (2024), static gestures provide faster classification and are less prone to errors compared to dynamic sequences, especially under constrained environments.
The most widely used pipeline for lightweight gesture recognition is MediaPipe, which relies on detecting 21 hand landmarks and passing them through a classifier. According to Random Nerd Tutorials (2023), MediaPipe can be successfully installed and executed on the Raspberry Pi, where it demonstrates acceptable real-time performance due to its efficient landmark detection strategy. This makes it a strong candidate for applications requiring responsive, on-device gesture recognition.
While MediaPipe is optimized for real-time detection, alternatives exist. Traditional approaches such as OpenCV-based contour and skin color detection remain useful for simple background-controlled setups. For instance, the HTIcodes (n.d.) open-source project demonstrates skin-color segmentation for gesture recognition using OpenCV, which requires less computational complexity but struggles with varying lighting conditions. More advanced pipelines use convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for gesture classification, achieving high accuracy on benchmark datasets. However, as reflected in Raksha, Bhavya, Divya, and Naseema (2025), CNN-based approaches typically demand greater computational power, making them less suitable for Raspberry Pi unless heavily optimized or paired with external accelerators.
Challenges remain in real-world deployment. As noted by Mohamed et al. (2024), issues such as cluttered backgrounds, varying lighting conditions, occlusion, and camera placement significantly impact recognition reliability. These limitations are especially relevant in uncontrolled environments where users interact from different distances and angles. When comparing the approaches, MediaPipe stands out for its balance between efficiency and accuracy. Random Nerd Tutorials (2023) reported that the pipeline achieves stable frames per second (FPS) on the Raspberry Pi while maintaining consistent tracking of hand landmarks. In contrast, CNN-heavy solutions may offer marginal accuracy gains but at the cost of significantly lower FPS, often rendering them impractical for real-time applications on resource-constrained hardware.
In light of these findings, the proposed system will adopt static gesture recognition using MediaPipe, with a design emphasis on clear shoulders-to-hand framing to improve robustness. Static gestures offer a simpler yet reliable interaction model, while MediaPipe ensures feasible performance on the Raspberry Pi platform. To further mitigate misclassifications, debounce logic will be applied so that gestures are only confirmed after consistent detection across multiple frames. MediaPipe offers landmark-based hand tracking with 21 key points per hand, allowing both static pose recognition and dynamic motion classification. Static gestures like “open palm” or “fist” are identified by analyzing spatial landmark relationships, while dynamic gestures (e.g., waving) rely on temporal sequence analysis. For FRAMES, static gestures were chosen due to their stability and lower processing requirements, achieving reliable real-time classification even under moderate lighting and varying camera angles.                   

Multimodal Fusion (Face + Gesture) & Gesture-Gated Logging
Multimodal fusion — the combination of two or more biometric or behavioral channels — improves authentication reliability by exploiting complementary strengths of each modality. As Khushboo Jha et al. (2024) explain, integrating facial features with a behavioural modality such as speech or gestures increases the system’s robustness against both environmental noise and spoofing attacks; in other words, fusion reduces false acceptance while preserving usability (Jha et al., 2024).
One pragmatic fusion design for attendance systems is sequential gating: first confirm identity via face recognition, then prompt for a confirming gesture within a short temporal window. As Mukthineni et al. (2020) showed in their face-authenticated hand-gesture system, tying a hand gesture to an already-recognized face reduces accidental activations and raises the effective security level because an attacker must spoof both modalities simultaneously (Mukthineni et al., 2020).
Different fusion strategies exist and are used depending on the application. Decision-level fusion (where each sensor issues a local accept/reject and the system combines these signals) is straightforward and well suited for constrained hardware; score-level and feature-level fusion are more computationally demanding but can provide higher discrimination if properly normalized. Bala, Gupta, and Kumar’s review (2021) summarizes these approaches and highlights how decision-level fusion often provides the best cost/benefit trade-off for embedded systems because it minimizes cross-modal preprocessing (Bala et al., 2021).
A second important design element is the temporal window and debounce policy. Practical literature recommends giving the user a short time window (commonly 1–2 seconds) to perform the confirming gesture after a face match, and requiring consistent detection across multiple consecutive frames before accepting the gesture (gesture debouncing). Sarma and Bhuyan (2021) discuss gesture spotting and suggest that robust gesture segmentation and debouncing are critical for avoiding spurious activations caused by random hand movements or passing people (Sarma & Bhuyan, 2021).
Empirical evidence indicates multimodal systems reduce error rates versus unimodal systems. For example, the AIP Advances study on feature-level fusion of face and speech reports improved FAR/FRR trade-offs and enhanced liveness detection when modalities are combined, particularly under noisy or partially occluded conditions (Jha et al., 2024). Similarly, earlier HCI research combining face and gesture authentication demonstrated that requiring both modalities substantially lowered false acceptances compared to face-only configurations (Mukthineni et al., 2020). These findings together support the view that adding a gesture confirmation stage is an effective countermeasure against “walk-by logging” and buddy-punching in attendance systems.
Operationally, the recommended pipeline for a Raspberry Pi–based attendance terminal is sequential and lightweight: (1) run face detection and quick embedding matching; (2) if recognition score exceeds a chosen threshold, open a 2-second window for gesture confirmation; (3) accept the gesture only after consistent detection across a small number of frames (debounce), and then write the log entry. This design prioritizes safety (minimizing false accepts), simplicity (decision-level checks are computationally cheap), and usability (short, clear gesture actions). The approach aligns with practical fusion guidance in the multimodal biometrics literature and with hand-gesture spotting recommendations in the gesture-recognition review (Bala et al., 2021; Sarma & Bhuyan, 2021).
 Based on the reviewed literature, this project will implement sequential gating (face → gesture) with a 1–2 second gesture window and a frame-based debounce requirement (e.g., consistent gesture across 3 consecutive frames). This choice is motivated by: (a) the demonstrated reduction in FAR with multimodal fusion (Jha et al., 2024), (b) the computational efficiency of decision-level fusion for Pi-class hardware (Bala et al., 2021), and (c) the robustness benefits of debouncing and temporal windows for hand-gesture spotting (Sarma & Bhuyan, 2021).
The effective recognition distance depends on camera placement, resolution, and lighting. Studies such as Swathi & RathnaChary (2023) and Tank et al. (2022) report optimal performance at 0.8–1.5 meters for Raspberry Pi setups, where facial features remain clear yet within field of view for gesture detection. Beyond this range, embedding vectors degrade due to low pixel density, reducing recognition accuracy. Controlled lighting conditions (500–800 lux) are ideal, though the system remains functional under most indoor environments with adequate illumination.

Real-Time Vision on Raspberry Pi (Performance & Optimization)
The Raspberry Pi 4B, powered by a quad-core ARM Cortex-A72 processor clocked at 1.5 GHz and up to 8 GB of RAM, is a popular platform for real-time vision applications because of its affordability and portability. However, as noted by Aboluhom and Kandilli (2025), its lack of a dedicated neural accelerator and reliance on general-purpose CPU cores necessitate careful optimization of deep learning models to achieve acceptable performance in tasks like face or gesture recognition. Without such optimizations, inference can be prohibitively slow, especially when running convolutional neural networks.
One of the most common strategies for model acceleration on the Pi is model compression. Quantization, as described in TensorFlow’s official guide (TensorFlow Lite, n.d.), reduces the numerical precision of model weights (e.g., FP32 to INT8 or FP16), leading to smaller models and faster inference with minimal loss of accuracy. Yin (2024) further explains that quantization can shrink models by up to four times and often improves latency by a similar factor, making it ideal for deployment on constrained devices. Similarly, pruning—where non-critical weights are removed—yields leaner models that still maintain predictive performance (Foucart, n.d.). Once compressed, models are typically converted to lightweight runtimes such as TensorFlow Lite (TFLite) or ONNX Runtime, which are specifically optimized for ARM platforms and support features like integer inference and hardware acceleration (ONNX Runtime, 2023; OpenVINO, 2023).
Beyond compression, system-level optimizations significantly impact performance. According to Tank, Patel, and Deshmukh (2022), experiments with MobileNet and SqueezeNet on Raspberry Pi 4 show that lowering input resolution, applying multi-threading, and enabling NEON vectorization yield substantial gains in frames per second (FPS). Their benchmarking reported that multithreaded pipelines—where separate threads handle camera capture, inference, and display—deliver much smoother performance than single-threaded approaches. Similarly, Aboluhom and Kandilli (2025) demonstrated that multitask learning models on the Pi reached near-real-time rates when properly optimized, proving the feasibility of deploying CNN-based recognition pipelines at the edge.
The choice of camera also influences latency and throughput. The official Raspberry Pi documentation (Raspberry Pi Foundation, n.d.) notes that the CSI-connected Pi Camera modules (v2 or HQ) generally offer lower latency and better synchronization with the GPU than most USB webcams, which often introduce additional driver overhead. For real-time attendance systems, this translates to fewer dropped frames and more consistent inference cycles.
Given these constraints and opportunities, the project sets explicit performance targets of ≥12 FPS throughput and ≤300 ms end-to-end latency per recognition cycle. These will be measured using a two-phase approach: (1) establishing a baseline with an unoptimized FP32 model in single-threaded mode, and (2) applying optimizations including INT8 quantization via TFLite, multithreaded execution, and resolution tuning. Metrics such as CPU utilization, memory usage, and FPS will be logged to ensure that the system meets real-time requirements on the Raspberry Pi 4B.
Beyond numerical performance, real-time visualization plays a key role in user experience. FRAMES implements a live feedback kiosk that shows recognition outcomes and gesture prompts instantly, ensuring transparency during attendance. Simultaneously, a web dashboard reflects user status through color-coded indicators (green for present, yellow for break). This dual visualization design bridges embedded recognition with centralized monitoring, making the system both responsive and interpretable.
Attendance / Access Systems: Modalities & Comparators
Attendance and access-control systems employ a variety of modalities, each with distinct operational characteristics. Token-based methods such as RFID (radio-frequency identification) use a passive or active tag that a reader detects; QR code systems rely on camera scanning of a displayed code; fingerprint systems use biometric fingerprint scanners; and facial recognition systems identify people based on facial features. Hybrid approaches combine modalities (e.g., face + gesture or face + token) to increase robustness and reduce fraud (Vadwala, 2024).
When comparing these modalities, several trade-offs emerge. Hygiene favors touchless solutions: RFID, QR, and face/gesture systems minimize physical contact, whereas fingerprint readers require direct touch and thus involve higher hygiene concerns (Hasini et al., 2024). Speed and throughput vary: RFID and QR check-ins are typically fast (sub-second to a few seconds), face recognition—depending on hardware and optimization—ranges from a fraction of a second to several seconds per check, and fingerprint scanners usually complete identification within 1–3 seconds but can slow down under high load or dirty sensors (Hasini et al., 2024; Vadwala, 2024). Spoof resistance differs markedly: token/QR systems are vulnerable to sharing or photocopying, fingerprint readers can be spoofed with fake molds unless liveness detection is included, and face recognition systems are vulnerable to photos or replay attacks if not bolstered by presentation-attack detection (PAD) or secondary confirmation such as gesture gating (Andrejevic & Selwyn, 2020; Niao et al., 2024). Finally, cost and maintenance considerations often determine modality choice: RFID terminals and QR setups are generally low cost per unit, fingerprint scanners fall in the mid-range, and face recognition systems require camera hardware plus compute resources for real-time processing — increasing total cost but potentially reducing human administration overhead over time (Vadwala, 2024).
Empirical deployments provide practical context. For example, Hasini et al. (2024) described an AI-based face attendance prototype where average time-per-check depended on camera, model, and network conditions; in their small-scale testbeds, recognition and logging typically took between 0.8–2.5 seconds per transaction depending on optimization. Reviews of institutional attendance systems indicate that token-based methods can produce very high throughput but are more prone to proxy attendance (“buddy punching”), whereas biometric systems reduce proxy risk but can create queues when throughput is limited or when user enrollment is complex (Vadwala, 2024; Niao et al., 2024). Human-factors literature similarly shows that fraudulent attendance remains a persistent operational problem in many organizations unless technological and procedural controls are combined (Niao et al., 2024).
Given these trade-offs and the classroom/lab context (moderate user density, need for hygiene, and limited per-terminal budgets), a face + gesture approach provides a balanced solution. As summarized by Vadwala (2024), multimodal solutions that combine biometric recognition with a secondary confirming cue reduce false acceptances and proxy attendance. Specifically, using face recognition as the primary identification channel and requiring a short confirmatory gesture (e.g., open palm to log in, closed fist to log out, a two-finger sign for break) enforces intentionality and deters both walk-by logging and proxy badge sharing. Gesture gating maintains touchless operation and adds minimal per-transaction time (typically under one second) when implemented as a short, static gesture and processed by a real-time hand-landmark detector (Mukthineni et al., 2020; Hasini et al., 2024).
In light of literature and practical constraints, the face + gesture modality is recommended for classroom and small-lab deployments. This approach preserves touchless operation, mitigates proxy attendance (by requiring intentional gesture confirmation), and can be implemented with a single camera per room (reducing hardware cost compared to multi-sensor PAD solutions). To keep throughput acceptable, the system should target quick, static gestures and optimized on-device inference (quantized models on Raspberry Pi 4) so that per-check latency remains around 1–2 seconds in practice. Operational policies (e.g., prompt enrollment, clear camera placement, and brief user guidance) will further improve acceptance and minimize queueing.

Web Dashboards and Digital Room Visualization 
Dashboards for attendance and occupancy management are designed to balance quick-glance awareness with the ability to drill down into detailed records. Common visualization patterns include per-room tiles that summarize occupancy at a glance, timeline views that display individual user activity across the day, and exportable reports for administrative use (Domo, 2025). Such patterns are widely used because they provide complementary perspectives: tiles allow administrators to monitor the real-time state of each room in seconds, while timelines and reports support deeper analysis of attendance behavior and long-term usage trends (Olowe et al., 2024; Vernon, 2022). These features are particularly useful in educational settings, where instructors or staff may need both an immediate view of classroom occupancy and archival records for audits.
At the backend level, two common frameworks are Flask and FastAPI, both of which are Python-based but differ in architectural design and throughput performance. Flask is synchronous and lightweight, making it a suitable choice for small-scale deployments with modest concurrency demands, such as single-classroom systems (Flask documentation, n.d.). In contrast, FastAPI is built around asynchronous request handling, enabling higher throughput and lower latency under concurrent loads, and is therefore often recommended for multi-room systems or scenarios requiring large numbers of real-time connections (Codecademy, 2025; FastAPI documentation, n.d.). Benchmarking and recent developer experiences indicate that while Flask remains easier to learn and integrate, FastAPI provides stronger support for modern practices such as WebSocket-based live updates, which are highly relevant to streaming attendance events directly to dashboards (Boadzie, 2025). Thus, backend selection can be guided by deployment scale: Flask is sufficient for lightweight pilot projects, whereas FastAPI offers better scalability and real-time responsiveness in multi-classroom deployments.
On the frontend, modern dashboards increasingly adopt component-based frameworks such as React, often combined with libraries like Bootstrap for layout or charting tools for visualization. React allows responsive, dynamic interfaces that update seamlessly when new attendance events are received, making it preferable over static server-side templating for real-time systems (Olowe et al., 2024). To support live updates, WebSocket connections can push new attendance logs directly to the dashboard, ensuring that room tiles and timelines update immediately when an entry, exit, or break event occurs. This is generally more efficient and user-friendly than polling, which introduces delays and unnecessary network overhead (Boadzie, 2025). The user experience should prioritize clarity and speed: administrators should be able to see, at a glance, which users are currently inside a room and which are on break, with the ability to click into an individual’s profile for a timeline of their movements throughout the day.
In the proposed system, the dashboard will be structured around a two-room layout since the pilot deployment targets two laboratory-classroom spaces. Each room is represented by a tile that lists its currently active occupants, with color-coded indicators reflecting status: green for present (after entry or break-in) and yellow for break (after break-out). This avoids misrepresentation of absence as a detected state, since the system only records actual entry, exit, and break transitions. Clicking on a user opens a detailed timeline view, where their day is visualized as a sequence of colored segments indicating time spent in-room, on break, or logged out. Administrators can filter the dashboard by user or room, and all data can be exported into CSV or PDF formats for reporting or archiving. In practice, the refresh cycle is managed via WebSocket streams, meaning changes appear in near real-time without manual refresh, while historical exports are generated on demand. By combining real-time visualization with exportable records, the dashboard design ensures that both operational monitoring and administrative reporting are supported in a unified system. 
Digital room visualization extends beyond simple data tables by providing spatial representations of classroom occupancy. Through web-based dashboards, FRAMES translates attendance data into interactive room tiles that dynamically change color and display real-time occupancy counts. This visual feedback aids both monitoring and spatial management, giving administrators immediate awareness of classroom status.
Data Management, Security & Privacy
A central design choice in biometric systems is whether to store raw images or vector embeddings. In most contemporary frameworks, embeddings are preferred because they are smaller in size, faster to compare, and less directly identifiable than raw facial images. These embeddings, often 128- to 512-dimensional vectors, represent unique but abstracted features of a face, reducing storage requirements while accelerating matching computations. Nevertheless, embeddings remain sensitive since inversion attacks can potentially reconstruct approximate facial images, underscoring the need for robust protection mechanisms (Jasmine & Jasper, 2022; Kruglov, 2019). Storing raw images, while useful for manual verification, poses greater privacy and legal risks; therefore, the system adopts an embedding-first strategy, with optional short-term storage of images strictly for audit and retraining purposes.
To protect this sensitive data, multiple layers of security are applied. At rest, records are encrypted using AES, ensuring that even if the database is accessed without authorization, the contents remain unintelligible. During transmission, TLS secures data streams between client, server, and dashboard, preventing interception. Access control is enforced through role-based access control (RBAC), limiting sensitive operations such as enrollment, deletion, or report export to administrators only. All actions are logged and auditable, allowing suspicious activity to be traced. These safeguards follow established IoT and edge computing security guidelines, which recommend encryption, strong authentication, and comprehensive monitoring as first-line defenses (OWASP IoT Top Ten, 2023).
Legal compliance is guided by the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act 10173) and its implementing rules. The Act emphasizes informed consent, requiring that individuals explicitly agree to the collection and processing of their biometric data (National Privacy Commission, n.d.). Purpose limitation is also mandated, restricting the use of attendance data strictly to logging and reporting, while data subject rights guarantee that individuals can request access, correction, or deletion of their records. Additionally, the law requires that organizations conduct Data Privacy Impact Assessments (DPIAs) to evaluate potential risks, and enforce retention policies that prevent indefinite storage of personal information (NPC IRR, n.d.).
In operational terms, the system adopts conservative safeguards to align with both security and privacy principles. Embeddings and logs are stored in a local database hosted on the Raspberry Pi, reducing exposure to external threats. Any temporary image captures are automatically deleted after 30 days, ensuring minimal retention. Only administrators are permitted to generate exports, which themselves are encrypted and pseudonymized before distribution. By combining embedding-based storage, encryption at rest and in transit, strict role-based controls, and adherence to RA 10173 provisions, the design ensures that attendance monitoring is not only accurate but also secure and legally compliant.
FRAMES ensures compliance with Republic Act No. 10173 by enforcing consent-based data collection, purpose limitation, and limited retention. Before enrollment, users are informed of how their biometric embeddings are used solely for attendance purposes. Data subjects retain rights to access, correct, or request deletion of their records. Retention is capped at 30 days for facial logs, after which records are automatically purged to prevent unnecessary data accumulation.

Software Quality Standards for System Evaluation (ISO/IEC 25010)
Software quality is increasingly evaluated through structured frameworks, and ISO/IEC 25010 stands out as a globally recognized model. According to ISO/IEC 25010:2023, the product quality model consists of nine key characteristics: Functional Suitability, Performance Efficiency, Compatibility, Interaction Capability, Reliability, Security, Maintainability, Flexibility, and Safety (ISO, 2023). These characteristics provide a consistent terminology and framework for specifying and evaluating system quality across industries.
Industry experts have summarized earlier versions of the model into more accessible forms. For example, Britton (2021) outlined the core quality characteristics and sub-characteristics, making the standard practical for developers and QA teams. Together, these sources ground our evaluation in a well-established international framework without requiring purchase of the full technical documentation.
For the purposes of this capstone project, prioritizing five ISO attributes that are most aligned with the real-time attendance system’s goals is the most practical way to evaluate the system: Functional Suitability, Performance Efficiency, Interaction Capability, Reliability, and Security. Functional Suitability will be assessed through recognition accuracy, false acceptance/rejection rates (FAR/FRR), and gesture-confirmed log entries. Performance Efficiency will be measured via frames per second (FPS), CPU and memory utilization, and system latency on Raspberry Pi hardware. Interaction Capability will be evaluated through user surveys and feedback, gauging ease of use, intuitiveness, and overall satisfaction. Reliability will be gauged through error rates, recoverability, and consistency of correct attendance logging. Security will be verified through implementation checks such as data encryption, authentication mechanisms, and alignment with data privacy requirements.
Other characteristics—namely Compatibility, Maintainability, Flexibility, and Safety—are acknowledged as important for long-term robustness, scalability, and deployment in larger environments, but are outside the immediate scope of this capstone due to time constraints and prototype-level implementation. By explicitly mapping selected ISO attributes to measurable system metrics, this study frames a focused yet rigorous evaluation strategy aligned with international quality standards.
TUP Prototype Evaluation Instrument
	In addition to software evaluation frameworks, prototypes in academic and institutional settings are often assessed using structured hardware evaluation instruments. At the Technological University of the Philippines (TUP), a standardized prototype evaluation system has been developed to rate student-built hardware on six critical criteria: aesthetics, workability, durability, economy, safety, and saleability (Technological University of the Philippines, n.d.). Each criterion is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, where 5 indicates “highly acceptable” and 1 indicates “not acceptable.” The overall acceptability is then interpreted by averaging scores across the six dimensions.
The six criteria cover multiple aspects of a prototype’s real-world potential. Aesthetics focuses on form factor and user interface clarity, ensuring the device is visually presentable. Workability ensures the system performs its intended function reliably, while durability assesses whether the device can withstand repeated or prolonged use in its target environment. Economy emphasizes cost-effectiveness and feasibility of production relative to its purpose. Safety evaluates compliance with standards for electrical safety and user protection. Finally, saleability measures potential adoption, considering whether the design has qualities that make it attractive for wider institutional or commercial use.
This framework is frequently adopted in TUP for assessing capstone prototypes and serves as a practical complement to software-oriented models such as ISO/IEC 25010. By applying the TUP instrument, this capstone will be evaluated not only on technical correctness (e.g., successful face and gesture recognition) but also on tangible prototype qualities such as design robustness, cost-efficiency, and usability. This ensures the project is reviewed in a holistic manner—acknowledging both its technical and practical readiness.

RELATED STUDIES
Local Studies
Several attendance monitoring systems have been developed in the Philippines, demonstrating early adoption of facial recognition and embedded systems. However, existing works reveal limitations in hardware efficiency, anti-spoofing, and multimodal fusion—gaps that this study addresses.

Tempus – A Facial Recognition Technology in Attendance Monitoring
Reynoso and Torres (2020) introduced Tempus, a Raspberry Pi 3-based attendance system using Haar cascade and LBPH for recognition, integrated with an IoT reporting feature. The study reported around 83% accuracy and favorable usability ratings among faculty and staff. While the system proved feasible in classroom settings, it suffered from modest recognition accuracy, no reported runtime performance, and absence of presentation attack detection (PAD) mechanisms (Reynoso & Torres, 2020). This capstone improves on such gaps by adopting a Pi 4 with FaceNet embeddings and gesture-gated PAD for stronger reliability.

Real-Time Class Attendance Monitoring using Smart Face Recognition
Delos Trinos et al. (2019) presented a prototype using “smart face recognition” deployed during the IEEE HNICEM conference in Laoag. The system leveraged real-time video capture to automatically log attendance. Although effective for controlled environments, the study did not address common Philippine constraints such as low lighting in classrooms or the potential for spoofing through photos and videos (Delos Trinos et al., 2019). These constraints motivate the inclusion of liveness cues and gesture gating in the present work.

YOLOv3 Inference Approach for Student Attendance
Alon et al. (2020) proposed a YOLOv3-based facial recognition attendance system to accelerate detection. The model improved detection robustness compared to older methods, but its implementation required higher-end computing resources, limiting deployment on cost-sensitive campuses with constrained hardware budgets (Alon et al., 2020). This capstone leverages a quantized MobileNetV2 backbone on a Raspberry Pi 4, striking a balance between speed and affordability for Philippine classrooms.

QSUM-eASys: A Face Recognition Attendance and Web-Based Monitoring System
	Domingo and Ladia (2024) developed QSUM-eASys for Quirino State University, integrating face recognition with a web dashboard for administrators. The system emphasized usability and centralized reporting but lacked measures for runtime performance evaluation and spoof prevention (Domingo & Ladia, 2024). By contrast, this study embeds PAD and multimodal gesture gating to prevent walk-by logging and strengthen data integrity.
	Across these local studies, three consistent gaps emerge: (i.) No PAD – none of the systems counter spoofing (e.g., photos, videos), (ii.) lack of gesture gating – attendance is logged as soon as a face is detected, risking false positives, and (iii.) limited runtime reporting – FPS, latency, and resource profiling are rarely documented. This capstone addresses these by implementing a quantized MobileNetV2 + FaceNet pipeline on Raspberry Pi 4, with gesture-gated PAD and integrated reporting dashboards suited to Philippine infrastructure constraints such as intermittent internet, variable lighting, and limited classroom budgets.

Foreign Studies
Facial Recognition Using Raspberry Pi
Several studies affirm the feasibility of this low-cost and portable platform for real-time attendance systems. Nadhan et al. (2022) introduced an automatic attendance monitoring framework that successfully reduced manual workload without significant loss of recognition accuracy. Similarly, Shabaneh et al. (2023) developed a Raspberry Pi-based classroom attendance system that emphasized edge processing and low power consumption, making it highly suitable for educational environments. Uddin et al. (2021) proposed an artificial intelligence-driven attendance monitoring system using facial recognition, confirming the real-time capacity of lightweight pipelines on embedded devices. Panwar et al. (2024) further advanced this line of work by integrating convolutional neural networks (CNN) for automatic face recognition-based attendance, which achieved higher accuracy rates but at the cost of increased computational demand. More recently, Elnozahy et al. (2025) extended Raspberry Pi applications to secure access control, while Aboluhom and Kandilli (2025) demonstrated its capability to handle multitask classification such as identity, age, and ethnicity recognition. Collectively, these works show that Raspberry Pi can sustain embedded recognition pipelines with adequate performance and scalability, though many overlook presentation attack detection (PAD), lighting variability, and runtime reporting, which this present study seeks to address.


Gesture Controlled Recognition Systems
     	Yadav and Jain (2024) demonstrated a vision-based hand gesture recognition model tailored for human–computer interaction that achieved high accuracy, proving its potential for real-time applications. Dhananjay et al. (2024) applied gesture-driven interfaces to smart home automation using Internet of Things (IoT) devices, showing that natural gestures could reliably control an ecosystem of appliances and systems. Complementing these findings, Muneeb, Rustam, and Jalal (2023) designed a gesture-based appliance automation framework for elderly assistance, which relied on wearables and machine learning models to provide responsive and dependable performance. Taken together, these studies highlight that gesture recognition can improve automation and interaction in technology-driven environments, particularly where touchless operation is needed. However, many of these approaches depend on resource-heavy algorithms or wearable sensors, while this study introduces lightweight MediaPipe-based gesture gating on Raspberry Pi for login, logout, and break functions, making the system both accessible and portable.

Multimodal Attendance & Fusion Systems
	Studies in this area emphasize how combining modalities enhances robustness against environmental constraints such as occlusion, noise, and spoofing. Mohammad, Jarullah, Al-Kaltakchi, Alshehabi & Dey (2024) proposed an embedded multimodal face recognition framework using MobileNetV2 and FaceNet on Raspberry Pi 400, achieving near real-time accuracy of around 12 frames per second while retaining portability. Another notable contribution is the work published in AIP Advances (2024), which explored feature-level fusion of face and speech modalities. This method improved recognition reliability in challenging conditions, although the approach required relatively heavy computational resources unsuitable for resource-constrained devices. Reviews of hand gesture recognition from 2023 and 2024 also stressed the efficiency of MediaPipe as a lightweight, real-time pipeline suitable for static and dynamic gesture tasks, thereby offering practical integration pathways in multimodal designs. In summary, multimodal approaches provide stronger resilience and security, but the computational trade-offs remain a challenge. The present study addresses this by combining facial recognition with gesture-based gating and PAD in an embedded Raspberry Pi system, balancing accuracy and resource efficiency.
	Through these studies, it becomes evident that international research confirms the technical viability of embedded, gesture-based, and multimodal systems for attendance monitoring. At the same time, gaps remain in areas such as spoofing resistance, low-light adaptability, and lightweight multimodal integration on embedded hardware. These lessons directly inform the innovations of this study, which merges Raspberry Pi-based face recognition with gesture gating and PAD to create a secure and cost-efficient attendance solution.
Operational Definition of Terms 
	            The operational definition of terms defines the necessary terms used in the study in the way they were used in the context of relation to the development of the study.
Attendance Tracking refers to the automated recording of time-in, time-out, and break logs of users, stored in the system’s database for reporting and monitoring.
  Break-In refers to the action performed by a user when returning from a break period. It is confirmed through gesture-based authentication after facial recognition, updating the system to mark the user as present in the room.
  Break-Out refers to the action performed by a user when leaving temporarily for a break. The system logs this state using gesture confirmation to differentiate between complete logout and temporary absence.
  Database refers to the storage system containing user profiles, attendance logs, and activity types such as login, logout, and break. It is secured through encryption and role-based access control.
  Embedded System is a computing setup where hardware (Raspberry Pi, camera, peripherals) and software (facial recognition, gesture recognition, dashboards) are integrated into a dedicated device that performs attendance and monitoring tasks.
  Face Embeddings are numerical vector representations of unique facial features extracted from an image, typically with 128 or more dimensions. These embeddings allow the system to compare and identify faces efficiently without storing full raw images.
  Facial Recognition refers to a biometric authentication process that identifies and verifies students and faculty based on unique facial features. In this system, it serves as the primary modality for identity validation.
  Gesture Debouncing is a technique used in gesture recognition where the system requires consistent detection across several frames before accepting an input, minimizing errors from random or unintentional hand movements.
  Gesture-Gated Logging refers to a multimodal security feature in which attendance is only recorded after both facial recognition and a confirming gesture are detected, preventing false or walk-by logs.
  Hand Gesture Authentication refers to a behavioral biometric process in which students and faculty perform specific hand gestures such as open palm, closed fist, two-finger peace sign, and thumbs-up to confirm attendance actions like logging in, logging out, and break logging.
  ISO/IEC 25010 Quality Model refers to the international software quality evaluation framework used in this study to assess system acceptability, focusing on Functional Suitability, Performance Efficiency, Interaction Capability, Reliability, and Security.
  Kiosk is a physical station or terminal where the Raspberry Pi, camera, and display are mounted. It serves as the interaction point for students and faculty to perform facial recognition and gesture authentication during attendance logging.
  MicroSD Card is the storage medium for the Raspberry Pi that holds the operating system, database, and system files necessary for running the FRAMES platform.
  Modules (Student, Faculty, Admin) refer to the subsystems of the web platform tailored to user roles. The Student Module refers to the part that displays personal attendance and break records. The Faculty Module refers to the part that allows viewing of student attendance per class and faculty logs. The Admin Module refers to the part that provides full system control, including user management, reporting, and monitoring.
  Multimodal Authentication is a security process that requires more than one biometric or behavioral input (e.g., face recognition combined with hand gestures) to validate user identity, reducing risks of spoofing or false logging.
  Occupancy Monitoring refers to the process of visualizing and tracking the number of users inside classrooms in real time through digital dashboards.
  Pi Camera Module is the official Raspberry Pi camera used in this study to capture real-time facial images and hand gestures. It provides better synchronization and latency performance compared to generic USB webcams.
  Presentation Attack Detection (PAD) refers to the security measures designed to prevent spoofing attempts such as photo or video-based facial recognition bypass. In this study, gesture confirmation acts as a PAD mechanism.
  Raspberry Pi 4 refers to a single-board computer serving as the main processing unit for facial recognition and gesture detection in this study, chosen for its affordability, portability, and compatibility with vision-based applications.
  Room Visualization is a feature of the web-based dashboard that provides a real-time graphical display of classroom occupancy, showing who is currently inside, on break, or logged out.
  Smart Monitoring System refers to the prototype designed and developed by the researchers, integrating Raspberry Pi–based facial recognition and hand gesture authentication, supported by a web dashboard for real-time attendance and occupancy monitoring.
  Web-Based Dashboard refers to the centralized platform developed in this study that provides real-time data visualization, reports, and user access for administrators, faculty, and students.

Conceptual Model of the Study
The Input-Process-Output diagram was used to show the conceptual model of the study as depicted in Figure 1.
Input
The input stage establishes the foundation of the system by identifying essential knowledge, software, and hardware requirements. These include familiarity with facial recognition techniques using OpenCV and dlib, hand gesture recognition through MediaPipe, and proficiency in Python, Flask, and ReactJS for system development. Considerations such as compliance with the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173), the use of agile development practices, and adherence to international standards like ISO/IEC 25010 are also part of the knowledge base. The software requirements consist of frameworks and tools such as Raspberry Pi OS, Python, OpenCV, MediaPipe, Flask or FastAPI, ReactJS with Bootstrap for the frontend, and MySQL or SQLite for database management. Hardware components consist of Raspberry Pi 4 (4GB or 8GB), Pi Camera, MicroSD (32GB+), kiosk setup, and basic peripherals to support system operations.
Process
This process explains how the system was developed through three stages: Design, Create, and Test & Improve.
Project Design: The researchers created dashboard layouts for students, faculty, and administrators. They also planned where to place the cameras and how the system components would communicate.
Create: This is the actual building phase. The backend and frontend were connected to the database, and the facial and gesture recognition modules were integrated.

Test & Improve: After building, several test runs were done to check accuracy and usability. Evaluations were based on ISO/IEC 25010 and the TUP Prototype Evaluation Instrument. Feedback helped the researchers fix bugs and improve performance.
Output
The output of this model is the Facial Recognition and Attendance Monitoring with Embedded System (FRAMES): A Web-Based System with Hand Gesture Control using Raspberry Pi, a web-based platform that combines facial recognition and hand gesture authentication using Raspberry Pi. It automates attendance, allows contactless logging, and shows real-time room data through a dashboard.
Evaluation
In this phase, the system is assessed for how well it performs in terms of usability, reliability, and efficiency. This ensures that the FRAMES meets both quality standards and the needs of the institution.
